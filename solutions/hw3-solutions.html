Subtlety worth flagging to students. np.where(... > 3, 'yes', 'no') treats a missing activity value as not > 3, so the ~273 students with a missing physically_active_7d are silently bundled into 'no'. Excluding them, the 'no' group is 5,855 rather than 6,128 — a good discussion point about how a one-line recode can quietly make a modelling decision for you.

95% Confidence Interval for Proportion¶

  • Calculate a 95% CI for those NOT active 3+ days. Do you get the same answer as proportion_confint()?
In [5]:
count = (yrbss['physical_3plus'] == 'no').sum()
nobs  = len(yrbss)
phat  = count / nobs

# --- by hand: p_hat +/- z * sqrt( p_hat (1-p_hat) / n ) ---
z  = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)                 # 1.96
se = np.sqrt(phat * (1 - phat) / nobs)
lo, hi = phat - z*se, phat + z*se
print(f"p_hat (not active 3+) = {phat:.4f}")
print(f"by hand 95% CI       : [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]")

# --- statsmodels ---
ci_low, ci_upp = proportion_confint(count, nobs, alpha=0.05, method='normal')
print(f"proportion_confint   : [{ci_low:.4f}, {ci_upp:.4f}]")

p_hat (not active 3+) = 0.4512
by hand 95% CI       : [0.4428, 0.4595]
proportion_confint   : [0.4428, 0.4595]

✅ Answer. By hand we get [0.4428, 0.4595], which matches proportion_confint(..., method='normal') to four decimals. They agree because method='normal' is the textbook normal (Wald) approximation: $\hat p \pm z_{0.975}\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. With n ≈ 13,600 the interval is very tight (±0.8 percentage points): we can be quite confident that just under 46% of students are not active on 3+ days. (Aside: proportion_confint also offers method='wilson', which is more accurate for proportions near 0 or 1 — not an issue here since $\hat p$ is near 0.5.)

Confidence Interval for Means¶

Calculate a 95% CI for the mean weight of participants who are physically active 3+ days a week and those who are not. Do you observe a difference?

weight_ci = (
    yrbss.dropna(subset=['weight'])
    .groupby('physical_3plus')['weight']
    .agg(['mean', 'std', 'count'])
    .assign(
        se       = lambda x: x['std'] / np.sqrt(x['count']),
        t_crit   = lambda x: stats.t.ppf(0.975, x['count'] - 1),
        moe      = lambda x: x['t_crit'] * x['se'],
        ci_low   = lambda x: x['mean'] - x['moe'],
        ci_high  = lambda x: x['mean'] + x['moe'],
    )
    .round(2)
)

Hypothesis Test: t-test¶

Is the difference in mean weight between those who exercise 3+ days and those who don't statistically significant? State the null and alternative hypotheses and use a t-test. We also provide the regression version to check they agree.

In [8]:
# H0: mu_yes - mu_no = 0   (no difference in mean weight)
# H1: mu_yes - mu_no != 0   (two-sided)

wy = yrbss.loc[yrbss['physical_3plus'] == 'yes', 'weight'].dropna()
wn = yrbss.loc[yrbss['physical_3plus'] == 'no',  'weight'].dropna()

t_stat, p_value = ttest_ind(wy, wn, equal_var=False)   # Welch
print("Welch two-sample t-test (weight: active vs not)")
print(f"  mean difference : {wy.mean() - wn.mean():+.3f} kg")
print(f"  t-statistic     : {t_stat:.3f}")
print(f"  p-value         : {p_value:.2e}")

Welch two-sample t-test (weight: active vs not)
  mean difference : +1.296 kg
  t-statistic     : 4.242
  p-value         : 2.23e-05

# --- Same test as a regression: weight ~ C(physical_3plus) ---
model = smf.ols('weight ~ C(physical_3plus)', data=yrbss).fit()
print(model.summary2().tables[1].round(4))

                            Coef.  Std.Err.         t  P>|t|   [0.025  0.975]
Intercept                 67.1878    0.2256  297.8766    0.0  66.7457   67.63
C(physical_3plus)[T.yes]   1.2962    0.3029    4.2791    0.0   0.7025    1.89

✅ Answer. Hypotheses. $H_0:\mu_{\text{yes}}=\mu_{\text{no}}$ versus $H_1:\mu_{\text{yes}}\neq\mu_{\text{no}}$.

Result. $t = 4.24$, $p \approx 2\times10^{-5}$ — we reject $H_0$. The mean weights differ, with active students about 1.30 kg heavier.

The regression is the same test. C(physical_3plus)[T.yes] has coefficient +1.30 (baseline = no) with $t = 4.28$, $p \approx 2\times10^{-5}$ — the slope is the difference in means, and its t-statistic matches the t-test (tiny differences come from Welch vs the equal-variance OLS standard error).

So-what — read this carefully. The result is statistically significant but the effect is small (1.3 kg on weights that range over 150 kg) and it runs opposite to the "exercise → lower weight" intuition. Two honest takeaways: (1) with n ≈ 12,600 almost any non-zero gap becomes significant, so report the effect size, not just the p-value; (2) this is observational — heavier teens may do more strength training (muscle weighs more), or activity and weight may be linked through many other factors. Significance here is not evidence that exercising makes you heavier.

✅ Answer. Spielberg averages 7.57 (95% CI [7.27, 7.87], n = 23); Burton averages 6.93 (95% CI [6.53, 7.33], n = 16).

Do the CIs overlap? Yes — just barely (Burton's upper 7.33 sits above Spielberg's lower 7.27).

Does the t-test agree? The Welch test gives $t = 2.71$, $p = 0.011$ — a significant difference of about 0.64 rating points.

This is the key teaching point. The two 95% CIs overlap, yet the difference is significant at 5%. The "overlapping CIs ⇒ no significant difference" rule of thumb is wrong (it is too conservative). The correct object is the CI for the difference in means, which here excludes zero. When two individual CIs overlap a little, you cannot conclude anything — you must test the difference directly.

95% (and 99%) confidence intervals "by hand"¶

Add standard error, t-critical, and CI endpoints to the summary table.

def mean_ci_table(df, group, value, conf=0.95):
    a = 1 - conf
    return (
        df.groupby(group)[value]
        .agg(['mean', 'std', 'count'])
        .assign(
            se      = lambda x: x['std'] / np.sqrt(x['count']),
            t_crit  = lambda x: stats.t.ppf(1 - a/2, x['count'] - 1),
            moe     = lambda x: x['t_crit'] * x['se'],
            ci_low  = lambda x: x['mean'] - x['moe'],
            ci_high = lambda x: x['mean'] + x['moe'],
        ).round(2)
    )

print("95% CIs\n", mean_ci_table(omega, 'gender', 'salary', 0.95)
      [['mean', 'se', 't_crit', 'ci_low', 'ci_high']], "\n")

Because both gender-related terms are individually insignificant, this interaction model is not strong evidence of differing slopes — treat it as suggestive. The right formal check is a joint F-test of the two gender terms against the experience-only model: sm.stats.anova_lm(model_exp_only, model4). Substantively, even the point estimates tell a benign story: any male advantage is at entry level and erodes with experience, the opposite of systematic discrimination against women.

✅ Answer — yes, the board member is right. Adding a squared experience term fits much better (R² 0.65 → 0.73), and the experience² coefficient is −17.5, highly significant ($p < 0.001$). A negative quadratic term means the salary–experience curve is concave: each extra year adds less than the one before. Concretely, from the quadratic the marginal value of a year is about £1,069/year around 3 years' experience but only ~£230/year around 27 years — exactly the pattern described (a 2→4 year jump is worth far more than a 26→28 jump).

How to model it. Two standard options: (1) add a quadratic term experience + I(experience**2) as above (easy to read, gives the turning point); or (2) model log(salary) so coefficients become approximately percentage effects (here ≈ 0.9% per year), which also captures diminishing absolute returns and often tames right-skew and heteroskedasticity. Either is a defensible "improved" specification; the quadratic answers the board member's question most directly.

Bottom line for the board. The raw £8,700 gap is real but almost entirely explained by experience: men in this sample are far more experienced, experience strongly predicts pay, and the gender term vanishes once it is included. The data therefore give no statistical evidence of pay discrimination conditional on experience — while leaving open the prior question of why female executives have so much less experience, which no salary regression can answer. As always, this is observational data, so every coefficient is an association, not proof of cause.
